Draw a pattern and this page writes a standard H.264 stream: your pattern, then one small program frame, repeated forever. Nothing on this page computes a generation. Your browser's video decoder computes every one of them, for as long as you leave the page open. In the tunnel, the board sits in the middle of a 1920 × 1080 picture that the decoder also zooms and turns, every frame, from its own previous frame. Each streak is the history of one cell on the board's edge, one notch per generation.

The whole machine

4032 × 832 · the same frames

The page decodes the whole frame and shows the top-left corner above. Here is all of it: 18 fields, each rewritten every frame by one weighted prediction. Board fields store the same number in all three colour planes, which a player shows as green (0) to magenta (255). The top-left corner is what a normal player shows.

What your decoder is fed

The page writes the first frames once, then hands your decoder the same 16 program frames over and over, through WebCodecs. The board lives in the decoder's reference frames. Nothing on the page keeps it.

How the decoder computes a generation

H.264 builds a B-frame macroblock by copying blocks from earlier frames and mixing them with explicit weights: clip(((w0·a + w1·b + 2L) >> (L+1)) + o). Each field below is a 200 × 200 board in its own region of the frame, rewritten every frame by one such mix, and motion vectors choose the cells it reads. One generation takes 6 frames, so at 60 frames a second the board advances 10 times a second. Flat out, it advances as fast as your decoder decodes.

Checked with the reference build: ffmpeg decodes every frame of these files to exactly what a simulation of these 18 ops predicts, sample for sample, and the board matches plain Game of Life every generation. Chromium decodes this page's stream to the same samples. Apple's decoder (QuickTime) plays the files too. If your browser shows something else, that is a decoder finding worth reporting.
